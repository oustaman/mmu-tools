Toolkit

Tools and websites for the fundamentals — layout, type, colour, access — and for collecting the exact numbers your deck asks for. Each card says which section of the deck it helps you fill.

Is my idea a good project?

Any theme can work, from any place or community, and your own project is welcome: a family business, a club you run, something nobody has built yet. Check it with your tutor before you settle it. Work you started for another module or a job is fine too, as long as it answers this brief. Need an idea? Theme ideas.

  1. Can you show the before? D1 needs a picture of every step people take now: the website, the form, the WhatsApp chat. If you cannot capture it, pick another.
  2. Is the audience a person you can picture? “Students” is everyone. “First-years who moved cities and have never registered with a GP” is someone.
  3. Is it one goal? One thing a person is trying to get done, in one sitting. Design that one flow, not the whole website: no About page, gallery or blog.
  4. Does it need more than a few screens? Three to six is plenty. If you need fifteen, the goal is too big; cut it in half.
  5. Do you care about it even a little? You will spend ten weeks on it. Something that annoyed you this term counts.

Steer clear of redesigning Spotify or Instagram (you end up critiquing them, with nothing of yours to mark); anything regulated, such as medical, legal or financial services, where steps exist because the law requires them; and adding features: your job is a shorter, clearer journey, not a longer one.

Settle it by week 3. You can change it later, but everything after is built on it, so a later change means more to redo.

When there’s no service yet

Your idea may have no website or app yet: a salon that books through Facebook messages and WhatsApp, a club that signs people up on paper, something nobody has built. It is marked the same as any other theme.

  1. The before is how people manage now. The message thread, the phone call, the paper diary, the spreadsheet: each one is a step. Write them into A2, and give D1 a screenshot or photo of each.
  2. Ask first, and blur. Ask the owner before you capture anything, and blur every other person’s name, number and photo. If you cannot use the real thing, recreate it with made-up names and say so in the caption.
  3. Asking how it works today is fine: that is finding out the process, not testing the design (why testing is out).
  4. Count both sides. Messages sent and replies waited for before; taps and fields after. That count is your evidence in C5.
  5. It can stay simple behind the screen. You design what a customer sees, not the system underneath. It is fine if the owner still confirms each booking by hand; say so in a caption.
  6. A labelled box is enough wherever a picture would go. Real photos of a business need the owner’s permission; generated ones go in A7.
  7. Branding is a choice. Use the business’s own logo and colours (say where they came from) or design new ones. Either way, A4 and A5 still need your sizes, codes and ratios.
  8. You do not have to launch it. Your clickable deck is the prototype. Whether anyone ever uses it is not marked.

C6 often has a ready answer here: the chat version keeps customers’ numbers and messages on a personal phone. What does your version collect, and what does it not need?

Using AI

AI is allowed. The brief permits it and calls it authentic industry practice, so using it is not cheating. Not using it is fine too, and costs you nothing.

Fine to use it for

  • exploring ideas and themes
  • mood board images
  • trying palettes, type pairings and layouts
  • placeholder text
  • feedback on your screens or your writing
  • fixing the language of your writing: grammar, spelling, clarity

What stays yours

  • The decisions: every number in A3–A5, and the reason for it.
  • The submission: a screen can start from an AI-generated layout, as long as you rework it and the whole deck follows the brief.
  • The argument in your rationale. AI may tidy its language; it does not make your case for you.

Say where you used it. Log each use in A7 as you go. Judge it in C8: was it any good, whose work is it, what did you change? Then D4, your AI statement: if you used it, how, where and why; if not, one line: No AI was used.

In the brief’s words: Submitting AI-generated text without declaration constitutes academic misconduct. Whether you used AI is not marked; what you make of it in C8 counts toward Critical Reflection.

Which tools?

Any you are comfortable with: PowerPoint, Google Slides, Figma, Canva, Adobe XD, or paper you photograph. The brief accepts all of them, and no software is taught or required. In this module your screens are slides, and linking them is your prototype (B8). Most tools below are free.

  1. The size of a box: with the inspector open, hover over part of the page. A label appears on it with its size in px — div.container 1140 × 820 is 1140 wide and 820 tall.
  2. The size of the screen: in device mode the current width × height is shown above the page. Drag the edge of the page to change it, or type a width in.
  3. Without device mode: in Chrome and Edge, keep the inspector open and resize the window — the page size shows in its top-right corner as you drag.

There is no Safari for Windows; use Chrome or Edge. More help: Chrome’s guide to device mode.

Collect the codes from any website — no install
  1. In Chrome or Edge, right-click the page and choose Inspect.
  2. Open the ⋮ menu in the panel → More tools → CSS Overview → Capture overview.
  3. You get every colour on the page, every typeface and font size, and a list of contrast problems — click any one to copy its code.
  4. Paste what you collected into A4 and A5, and say where it came from.
