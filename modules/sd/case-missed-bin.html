Reporting a missed bin

Liverpool City Council · walked on 20 October 2026 on a phone and a laptop, with made-up details, stopping before anything was sent · the “now” screens are redrawn from that walk, because the council publishes no licence for its pages · see the live page ↗

WhoA Liverpool resident whose bin was not emptied this morning — often on a phone, at the kerb, with the bin still full.
GoalGet it emptied before the next collection, which means reporting it by 2pm on the next working day.
The whole jobSay which bin, and where. The council already knows the rest.

Now · screen 2 of 6

Which bin? Two different names for it

The first question of the form is the right one — but it is asked in words the previous page did not use.

  1. 4Colours on one page, categories on the nextThe information page talks about blue, purple and green bins. The form says Refuse, Recycling, Garden waste. A resident has to translate one into the other.
  2. 5Choosing a bin turns it blackThe recycling bin is blue until you choose it; then it turns black on yellow. The colour you recognised it by disappears at the moment you pick it.
  3. 6Next will not press, and nothing says whyNext stays faded until you also pick a type of bin from the menu below. No message says so.

Now · screen 6 of 6

What the form never checks

  1. 13The deadline is left to the residentThe 2pm rule decides whether a report is any use, but the form never asks when the bin was due — so it cannot tell you that you are too late, or when it will come back.
What already works — keep it. Atkinson Hyperlegible, a typeface drawn for readers with low vision, at 21–22px. Every text colour clears 6.6:1. A progress bar, and a postcode look-up instead of typing an address. The problems are in the journey, not the look.

What happened to every step

Nothing just disappears: each step now is kept, merged, moved or removed — with a reason.

Step nowWhat happenedWhy
Information pageMerged into a 38-word start pageOnly the deadline decides whether reporting is any use; the other rules are one link away
Choose your bin, and its typeMoved after the address; type removedThe address tells the council which bins are due and what kind they are
Name, email, phoneMerged into one optional contactNothing in emptying a bin needs a name; one way to send an update is enough
Postcode and addressKept, moved firstEverything else follows from it
Additional informationRemoved from the main pathRarely needed; if the council wants it, it returns as an optional box on the bin screen
Assisted collectionRemovedArranged with the council, so already on file for the address. A step the council needs never disappears — this one was already answered
SummaryKept, with Change links and one buttonFixing a mistake should not mean starting again
FinishKept as “Send report”, plus a confirmationThe button says what it does, and the next screen says what happens

The redesign assumes the council can look up which bins are due at an address — its public bin-day look-up does this today — and that its case system does not insist on a name or the assisted-collection answer. If it does, they return as one optional question.

What a marker would credit, and where it goes in your deck

Every claim has a number“199 words → 38”, “10 things to fill in → 4”. Saying “simpler” is a description; counting is a defence.
The order is a decision, and it is explainedAddress first, because the council can answer the rest — argued from the task, not from taste.
It keeps what works, and says soThe typeface and colours stay, with the reason. Knowing what not to change is a design decision too.
Accessibility is built inColour never works alone, every field says what it is, every text clears 6:1, one question per screen.
An ethics answer that is specificName and phone are no longer collected because nothing needs them — data you do not hold cannot leak.
A cross-cultural point that is realBin colours mean different things in different places: colour with a name, never colour alone.
Every step is accounted forKept, merged, moved or removed, each with a reason.
It states its limitsIt says what the redesign assumes about the council’s systems. A case that names its limits is trusted more.
