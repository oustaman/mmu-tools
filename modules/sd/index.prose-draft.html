01

How your week works

The same three slots every week, so you never have to check what is happening. One hour together, two hours making something, two hours with the door open.

Lecture1 hr
Whole cohort · lecture theatre

One idea, demonstrated live. There is no slide deck — the tool we are using is the exhibit, and you will use the same one yourself two hours later. It ends by naming exactly what the workshop will produce.

Come to this one
Workshop2 hrs
Three groups of ~33 · breakout rooms

You work in the week's instrument and leave with one finished piece of your portfolio. All three rooms run the identical sheet, so it does not matter which room you are in or who is standing at the front.

Come to this one
Studio2 hrs
Same rooms · staffed, nothing taught

Nothing is taught and nothing is missable. The room is open and a tutor is in it. Book a ten-minute check-in, or just work here, or don't come. Nothing is delivered in this slot that you could miss and be disadvantaged by.

Optional — but see below

The studio hours are where the marks actually move

Ten-minute check-ins run against a rota — twelve slots per room per week, which across the semester is roughly three booked check-ins each, landing near weeks 3, 6 and 9. Sign up in advance.

A check-in is not a tutorial and not a critique. You open your spec sheet, we ask what is blank and why, and you leave with one thing to fix, written down. Ten minutes. The students who use these do not arrive in January with a problem that took five weeks to become unfixable.

Two weeks that are not like the others

Week 6 is a full teaching week, not a reading week — it is the tools-and-AI session, and it lands there because that is the point in the semester when you have enough declared decisions for AI to be useful rather than decorative.

Week 11 is deliberately dead. No lecture, no workshop, studio only, for submission problems — exports, PDF assembly, Moodle. If your work is done you do not need to be there.

02

What you hand in

One portfolio, as a single PDF, worth 100% of the module. About 2,000 words' equivalence in three parts. You do not build it in December — every workshop produces one component of it, so by week 10 it is assembled rather than started.

Part A — Design spec sheet≈ 400 words equivalent · structured fields
  1. Theme & audience — one sentence each
  2. Screen inventory — 6–8 screens, each with a named job
  3. Layout — columns, gutter, margin, max width, three breakpoints, spacing scale
  4. Typography — families and roles, sizes, line heights, scale ratio, measure
  5. Colour — hex values with roles, plus a contrast ratio for every text/background pair
  6. Accessibility — minimum touch target, focus state, non-colour cues
  7. AI use log — tool, what it produced, what you changed, why
Part B — The workvisual · no word count
  • Multi-screen prototype — 6–8 screens, plus two key screens shown at all three of your declared breakpoints
  • Supporting artefacts — concept sketches, wireframe set, mood board, style tile
  • One annotation per artefact — about 100 words: what it is, and what it decided
Part C — Reflective rationale500–700 words · six sections
  1. Theme & audience — who, what they need, why ~80 w
  2. Hierarchy & layout — what you wanted read first ~120 w
  3. Typography — why this pairing, scale, measure ~120 w
  4. Colour — what the contrast figures made you change ~120 w
  5. Accessibility & users — one decision, and its cost ~120 w
  6. Next — what you'd change, on what evidence ~80 w

The rule that decides most of your mark

Every section of Part C must cite at least one declared value from Part A. Not "I chose a warm palette because it felt friendly" — "I moved the body text from #8B8382 to #56504F because at 16px on #F5F4F2 the first pair measured 2.9:1 and failed AA."

That is the whole difference between describing a design and defending one, and it is the thing this module is actually assessing. It is also checkable, which is why it is the rule.

On AI, plainly

Use whatever tools you like. Figma, Figma AI, generative image tools, anything. Tool skill is not what is being marked, and pretending otherwise at Level 4 in 2026 would be theatre.

What you must do is log it — Part A §7: the tool, what it produced, what you changed, and why. A generated screen can be excellent. It just has to be owned, and owning it means being able to say what you decided and what the machine decided.

The honest version of this: if you can generate a competent-looking screen in a minute, then "does this look good" has stopped measuring anything. So the marks sit on the parts a generator cannot supply for you — a type scale your own specimens obey, contrast ratios that match the hex values they came from, and a rationale citing your own numbers.

05

Where the examples come from

There is no slide deck because there are three public reference buildings instead. They are cited in the lectures and you can walk them yourself — they hold real, sourced material rather than screenshots of things somebody liked.

Interfaces Institute Ten principles, each shipping two working interfaces — one that breaks it and one that keeps it, both timed. Plus twenty-two collections of interaction evidence and a dictionary of 787 terms. interfaces.institute · weeks 1, 2, 6, 7, 8, 9 Written Institute The Type Laboratory, the Hall of Typologies and an atlas of 42 script plaques. The whole building is set in one typeface — restraint demonstrated rather than asserted. written.institute · weeks 3, 4 Symbolic Institute Twelve visual-grammar entries — traffic colour, hazard diamonds, the hamburger, the arrow — and eleven collections, each marked with how well evidenced it is. symbolic.institute · weeks 2, 5, 6, 7, 10

Two things worth knowing about those buildings

Every claim on them carries a marker saying how well evidenced it is — documented, traditional or folk. Miller's 7±2 is filed as folk, on a site that also explains why people keep quoting it. That habit is what week 10 is teaching you to do in your own rationale.

They also publish their own corrections. The Interfaces Institute has a page listing every change it has made, including the ones where it was wrong. You are allowed to cite them; you are also allowed to catch them out, and that is worth more.
