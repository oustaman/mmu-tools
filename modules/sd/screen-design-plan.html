01

What changed, and what it forces

Four things about this year's delivery break the model UXCI runs on. Each one has a consequence that shapes everything downstream.

ChangeConsequence
Five hours a week, split 1 + 2 + 2 across a lecture theatre and three breakout rooms Three tutors deliver the same workshop simultaneously. The session plan can no longer live in one person's head — it has to be a document a colleague can open and run cold.
100+ students, one marker Marking has to be triaged, not endured. That means the portfolio must contain structured, declared values a machine can check before a human looks at anything.
No software teaching — students use Figma, Figma AI, generative image tools, whatever they like Tool skill stops being assessable, which is correct at Level 4 anyway. What remains assessable is judgement: the decisions, their internal consistency, and the justification.
Slides replaced by instruments The lecture hour becomes a live demonstration in a tool the students then use themselves in the workshop. Build the instrument once; it serves as exhibit, worksheet and marking evidence.

The thing generative tools actually break

If a student can generate a competent-looking screen in a minute, then "does this look good" has stopped measuring anything you wanted to measure. The assessment below moves the weight onto the parts a generator cannot supply on the student's behalf: a declared type scale that its own specimens obey, contrast ratios that match the hex values they came from, and a rationale that cites the student's own numbers. A generated screen can still be excellent — it just has to be owned, and owning it means being able to say why every value is what it is.

The weekly shape

Same three slots every week, so students and staff stop having to check what's happening. Weeks 1–10 are content; week 11 is submission surgery.

1hWhole cohort
You

Lecture

One idea, demonstrated live in the week's instrument. No deck — the tool is the exhibit. Ends by naming exactly what the workshop will produce.

Lecture theatre · 100 cap
2h3 × ~33
You + 2 colleagues

Workshop

Students work in the instrument and leave with one finished portfolio component. Runs from a one-page sheet, identical in all three rooms.

3 breakout rooms · parallel
2h3 × ~33
Staffed, unscheduled

Studio

Nothing taught. Room open, tutor present, booked ten-minute check-ins running against a rota. Everyone else works or leaves.

Same rooms · drop-in + rota

The studio hour is the part that pays for itself. Weekly formative contact is what stops a hundred portfolios arriving in January with the same five fixable problems — which is the actual cause of marking burnout, more than the volume is.

Week 6 and week 11

Because check-ins now happen every week in the studio slot, week 6 no longer needs to be a teaching gap — it becomes a full content week, and the plan below uses it for the tools-and-AI session, which is where students are ready for it. Week 11 stays dead by design: no lecture, no workshop, studio only. Attendance will be low and that is fine; the work should be done by then. Put nothing in it that a student could miss and be disadvantaged by.

Why the spec sheet is the important one

Build it first, even before the playgrounds. Every other instrument writes into it, students see their portfolio filling up week by week, and it produces the one artefact that makes automated first-pass feedback possible at all. If only one thing gets built this summer, build this.

Week by week

Every workshop ends with a component in the portfolio. By week 10 the portfolio is assembled rather than started — which is the only version of this that works with a hundred students.

Week 1

Lecture — Why screens look the way they do

From manuscript margin to print grid to screen. The claim: every layout decision you'll make was made before, for reasons, and those reasons still hold. Live teardown of three screens.

Workshop — Screen teardown

Three screens each in the Hierarchy Teardown, marking reading order and naming the device doing the work. Ends with a theme shortlist.

Output3 teardown sheets
Theme shortlist (3)
Week 2

Lecture — Composition and visual hierarchy

Scale, weight, space, alignment, proximity. Gestalt as a working tool rather than a list. What a focal point costs and why you only get one.

Workshop — Thumbnail studies

Eight rough compositions for a single screen, on paper, ten minutes each. No software, deliberately. Peer swap and rank at the end.

Output8 thumbnail studies

Lecture — The grid

Columns, gutters, margins, and the spacing scale that stops every gap being a guess. Why a grid is a decision you make once so you don't remake it four hundred times.

Anatomy, classification and pairing; measure, leading and the fact that most bad screen type is a line-length problem wearing a font problem's clothes.

05

The studio hours

Two hours, three rooms, roughly 33 students per room, weeks 2 through 10. Nothing is scheduled — but the time is structured, or it evaporates.

ElementDetail
Booked check-ins12 × 10-minute slots per room per week, signed up in advance. Across nine studio weeks that's 108 slots against 33 students — roughly three booked check-ins each, landing near weeks 3, 6 and 9.
Everyone elseOpen studio. Work in the room, use the tutor when the queue is empty, or leave. Attendance is not the point; availability is.
The check-in itselfOpen their spec sheet. Ask what's blank and why. Ten minutes, one thing to fix, written into the decision logger before they leave. No feedback essays.
EscalationAny student who misses two booked slots gets flagged into the engagement monitor rather than chased individually.

Protect the boundary

Five hours of contact plus a hundred students is already at the edge. The studio slot works only if it stays genuinely unplanned teaching — the moment it acquires content, three staff are preparing five hours a week instead of three, and the model collapses. If a colleague asks what to prepare for studio, the answer is nothing.

06

Workshop run sheet

One page per week, identical structure, written so a colleague who has not seen the lecture can run the room. This is the artefact that makes three parallel groups survivable.

TimePhaseWhat the tutor does
0:00–0:05SetRead the framing paragraph aloud. It's written on the sheet. It restates the lecture's one idea and names today's output.
0:05–0:15DemoOpen the instrument on the projector, work one example to completion. The sheet lists the exact clicks.
0:15–1:20WorkStudents work. Tutor circulates. The sheet lists the three questions this task usually generates, with answers.
1:20–1:40SwapPairs exchange work against a two-line check printed on the sheet. Not a critique — a can-you-tell-what-this-is test.
1:40–1:55FixAct on what the swap surfaced.
1:55–2:00ExportEveryone exports into the spec sheet before leaving. Tutor confirms the room is empty of unexported work.

Each sheet also carries: Done looks like — three bullets a tutor can check at a glance; If they're stuck — the two most common blocks and the unblock; If they missed last week — the minimum catch-up, because with a hundred students someone always did.

Where "responsive" now sits

None of the three outcomes mentions responsiveness, so it cannot be a marking criterion on its own — but it can stay in the task, which the brief controls. Keep the requirement for two key screens at three declared breakpoints, and mark it under LO1 as evidence that the grid decision holds up rather than as a separate thing being assessed. That keeps the brief inside the approved outcomes while preserving the teaching, and the spec's own reference to "emerging formats, such as web and mobile" is the justification if anyone asks at moderation.

Changes from your current draft

  • LO list replaced with the spec's three, verbatim. Criteria map to those and only those.
  • "Responsive" narrowed to two key screens at three declared breakpoints, and reframed as task rather than criterion, per the note above.
  • The spec sheet is new. It converts the portfolio's invisible decisions into declared values.
  • AI disclosure is required, as a log rather than a statement, and is assessed as part of the rationale's honesty rather than policed separately.
  • Word budgets are per-section, matching the UXCI approach that made LLM pre-checking work there.
08

Marking at scale

The honest constraint: an LLM cannot tell you whether a design is any good. It cannot see hierarchy in a PDF reliably, it will praise generated mediocrity, and it has no taste. So don't ask it to. Ask it only about consistency, completeness and reasoning — where it is genuinely reliable, and where most of the formative feedback students need actually lives.

Give the machine this

  • Are all seven spec-sheet sections present and populated?
  • Do the declared type sizes actually follow the declared ratio?
  • Do the stated contrast ratios match the stated hex pairs — and do any fail AA?
  • Do the wireframe labels match the screen inventory?
  • Are three breakpoints declared and all three shown?
  • Does each rationale section cite a declared value, or is it generic?
  • Word budgets per section; is the AI log filled in?
  • Does the rationale contradict the spec sheet anywhere?

Keep this yourself

  • Is the composition any good?
  • Is the type pairing appropriate to the theme?
  • Does the palette do what the student claims it does?
  • Is the reasoning genuine or fluent-sounding filler?
  • Craft, restraint, and whether the thing holds together
  • Anything that decides a boundary between grade bands

The sequence

  1. Automated pre-check runs on every submission and produces a completeness-and-consistency report. This is formative — release it to students, and run it in week 10 too, before submission, so the fixable problems get fixed rather than marked.
  2. You mark Part B and the quality of Part C, with the pre-check report open beside you. The mechanical questions are already answered, so your attention goes where only it can go.
  3. Moderation sample drawn as usual — the existing moderator and consistency-checker tools already handle this.

Two cautions worth writing down now

The pre-check is not a grade and must never be described as one — to students, to colleagues, or in the Moodle wording. It reports whether required things are present and internally consistent. A portfolio can pass every check and be weak work.

Check the assessment regulations before any student-facing text mentions automated processing. Formative self-check tooling that students run themselves is uncontroversial; anything that reads as automated marking is a different conversation with the faculty, and it is much easier to have it in advance than retrospectively.
