Thinking in wireframes

Today you start turning problems into screens, before there is any question of how they look. We will wireframe one whole journey, six screens, and meet the first building blocks on the way. The rest of the blocks come in week 7.

The idea

A wireframe is an argument about priority

It says: this comes first, this comes second, and that does not belong here at all. Put colour and type on it, and people start talking about the colour and type.

Everything on the right that is not on the left was decided without asking whether the screen works. At this stage, that is time spent on the wrong question.

The rule

What you decide now, and what can wait

Now

  • What this screen is for: its job
  • What is on it, and what is not
  • What comes first
  • The real words
  • Where it goes next

Not yet

  • Fonts
  • Colours
  • Icons and photographs
  • Shadows, rounded corners, gradients
  • Anything you would call “the look”

If you are choosing a font, stop. You have left the wireframe. Your type and colour decisions from week 4 come back when you finish the screens.

Two ways to do it: both count for B5

A pen, or the Screen maker

Sketch. Pen and paper, then a photo.

Screen maker. Blocks that snap into rows.

  • Sketching is faster for trying several versions. Use a marker, so you cannot draw small details.
  • The Screen maker is tidier, and the words are easy to change. Copy each screen into your deck as a picture.
  • Mix them. Sketch three ideas, then build the one that works.

Boxes and labels either way. A box with a cross through it means “a picture goes here”. You never need to draw the picture.

The building blocks

Organisms: one unit, doing one job

A button on its own does nothing. A question with its answers does a job: it finds out one thing. Brad Frost’s atomic design calls that an organism: small parts working together as one unit.

So you do not build screens from buttons. You build them from organisms. There are in the set, and you will meet just thirteen today.

Each has a code, a name and a job. This one is ANS-02, One answer from a few: it asks a question with one answer from a short list.

The rules of the grid

A screen is a budget of 14 rows

  • A phone screen here is 375 px wide, and every organism is that wide.
  • Each organism is a whole number of rows, and a row is 56 px. So any of them fits under any other.
  • Below the status bar there are 14 rows: 784 px.

That limit is the point. A screen with room for everything has decided nothing. When the rows run out, something has to go, or it has to move to another screen.

Working on a website for big screens? The Screen maker has a Desktop setting: the same rows, across 12 columns.

              All six

              One journey, thirteen organisms

              Six screens, and organisms placed, but only thirteen different ones. That repetition is a system, and it is what makes a service feel like one thing.

              One habit

              Use the real words, not placeholders

              The left one could be anything. The right one can be wrong, and that is what makes it useful: you can read it and disagree.

              Real words change the layout. If an answer will not fit on one line, you want to know now. In the Screen maker, click any words and type. On paper, write them out.

              How to check it

              The blind test

              1. Hand your screens to a partner, in order.
              2. For each one, they say what its job is, and what they would do next.
              3. Any screen they cannot explain, redraw now.

              That is all the checking you need today. You are testing whether the screens make sense to someone else. You do not need to find users or run a study.

              Today

              Your turn: answer your worst ✗

              1. Look at your flow. Which ✗ costs people the most?
              2. What two or three screens would answer it? Give each a job.
              3. Wireframe them, by sketching or in the Screen maker. Quick rather than tidy.
              4. Blind test them with a partner, and redraw what did not work.
              5. Put them in your deck, in B5. That is the start of your set.
