Stuck for a theme?

Press the button for a theme that fits the brief: someone specific, one thing they are trying to get done, how they do it today, and the screens your version would need. Each idea also comes with a starting point for the two paragraphs the brief asks for by name: ethics or sustainability (C6) and a cross-cultural consideration (C7). Treat it as a starting point. Change the audience, the place, the goal. It becomes yours the moment you change something. Got your own idea already? See your own idea, or a real business.

Before you lock it in week 3
  1. Can you screenshot the before? D1 needs one picture of every step people take now: the website, the form, the WhatsApp chat. If you cannot capture it, pick another.
  2. Is the audience a person you can picture? “Students” is everyone. “First-years who moved cities and have never registered with a GP” is someone.
  3. Is it one goal? One thing a person is trying to get done, in one sitting. An app that does everything has no hierarchy to write about.
  4. Does it need more than a few screens? Three to six is plenty. If you need fifteen, the goal is too big; cut it in half.
  5. Do you care about it even a little? You will spend ten weeks on it. Your own life is the best place to look: something that annoyed you this term counts.

Your own idea, or a real business

A project of your own is welcome — a family business with no website, a club you run, something you have noticed nobody has built. Say a mum’s salon takes bookings through Facebook messages and WhatsApp, and you design the website where customers book a slot instead. The brief asks the same of it as of any other theme. In practice:

  1. Design one flow, not the whole website. Pick the one goal — book a slot — and design the three to six screens it takes. No About page, no gallery, no blog. A2 lists those screens and nothing else.
  2. The before is the chat. The Facebook message, the WhatsApp back-and-forth, the paper diary, the confirmation text: each one is a step, and D1 gets a screenshot or photo of each. Ask the owner first, and blur every customer’s name, number and photo. If you cannot use the real chat, recreate it with made-up names and say so in the caption.
  3. Count both sides. Messages sent and replies waited for before; taps and fields after. That count is your evidence in C5 — you do not need anyone to try it.
  4. Do not test it with customers, family or friends. Showing work to people as evidence needs ethics approval, which this assessment does not have. Asking the owner how bookings work today is fine: that is finding out the process, not testing the design.
  5. It can stay simple behind the screen. You are designing what a customer sees, not the system underneath. It is fine if the owner still confirms each booking by hand — the request comes from your website instead of a chat. Say so in a caption.
  6. No need to make every image. A labelled box — photo of the salon — is enough wherever a picture would go. Real photos of the business need the owner’s permission; generated ones go in your AI log, A7.
  7. Branding is a choice. Use the business’s own logo and colours (say where they came from) or design new ones. Either way A4 and A5 still need your type sizes, colour codes and contrast ratios.
  8. You do not have to launch it. Your clickable deck is the prototype. Whether the business ever uses it is not marked.

C6 has a ready answer here: the WhatsApp version leaves customers’ phone numbers and messages on a personal phone. What does your version collect, and what does it not need?
