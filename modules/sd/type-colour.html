Colour and type speak first

Before anyone reads a word, the colours and the typeface have already said something. Four slides on what colours mean here, in Britain, then four on how one sentence changes voice with its typeface.

Colour in a British context · 3 of 4

Green and yellow: go, and careful

🏃 Fire exitsafe, this way
♻ Recyclingand anything “green”
No parkingdouble yellow lines
⚠ Cautionhi-vis, warning signs

Green for success and yellow for warning will be read the way you mean them, but never by colour alone: about 1 in 12 men cannot tell red from green. Add a word or an icon (that is A6), and keep yellow for fills, never for text.

Colour in a British context · 4 of 4

What this means for A5 and C4

  • Give each colour a job, not a mood: text, button, error, success, accent.
  • Ask what it means to your audience, here or wherever they are.
  • Check the ratio of every colour that sits behind or under text.

C4, in one line: “Errors are #B3261E: red reads as stop to a British audience, and it is 6.5:1 on white.”

Nothing in the words changed. The typeface, its weight, its colour and the space around it decided whether it is a threat, a welcome, a goodbye or a fact.

Type · 4 of 4

What this means for A4 and C3

  • Readable first. A service is read in a hurry, on a phone, outdoors: the voice comes second.
  • One voice, used everywhere. One or two typefaces, a few sizes, each with a job.
  • Say why, with a number. Who reads it, where, and on what decides the size.

C3, in one line: “Body text is 18 px Atkinson Hyperlegible, because people report a missed bin on a phone at the kerb.”
