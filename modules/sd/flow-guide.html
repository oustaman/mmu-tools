How flows work

A flow is a journey drawn as boxes, in the order a person meets them. It does two jobs in your deck. In week 5 it shows how people do it now, and where they get stuck. In week 7 it shows your version, and what happened to every step on the way. This page explains each option in the Flow maker with a real example from the four cases, then lets you test yourself.

Part 1 · Drawing how it works now

Start with what really happens, not what the organisation says should happen. Walk it yourself, and write a row for everything the person does, in their order.

Step

Anything the person does that moves them on

Reading a page, filling in a form, making a call, sending a message. Each one gets a box, numbered in order.

Ask: would the person say “then I…”? Then it is a step. Make it one box per page, call or message. “The website” is too big; “click Next” is too small.

From the missed bin case: the council’s report starts with an information page, then a form that asks for the bin, then your details.

Where

The small line under each step: where it happens

Website, form, phone, message thread, shop counter. Write it in the second box of the row.

Why it matters: every change of channel is a cost you can count. Four channels to place one order is a finding in itself.

From the bakery case: website, a scanned menu, a phone that rings out to a full voicemail, then messages. After the first two channels, still nothing can take an order.

Wait

Time when the person can do nothing

Waiting for a reply, an email or a delivery. It is not a step, because there is nothing to do, so it has no number. But it is counted, because it is often the longest part of the journey.

Ask: is the person waiting for someone else? Then add a wait, and say how long.

From the bakery case: a message sent on Monday at 12:44 gets its first reply on Wednesday, 46 hours later. By then Saturday is fully booked.

✗ Stuck

A step where people go wrong

The path is there, but people get lost, give up, or make a mistake. Press ✗ stuck on the row and say why, with a number if you have one. These are the problems your version has to answer.

Ask: where would someone stop, guess, or have to ask? Each place is a ✗. Write the reason as a fact, not an adjective: “199 words before you can start”, not “too much text”.

From the missed bin case: the information page talks about blue, purple and green bins, but the form says Refuse, Recycling, Garden waste. Next will not press, and nothing says why.

Yes/no question

A point where the answer sends people different ways

Drawn as a diamond. “Yes” carries on along the row. For “no”, write what happens, then choose where it leads: stuck, or back to an earlier step.

Ask: does the answer change where the person goes next? Only then is it a yes/no question. If everyone goes to the same next screen, it is a step, even when it ends in a question mark. “Which bin was not emptied?” is a step: every answer leads on to the same screen.

From the Railcard case: the ID number and the photo are first asked for after choosing a card, agreeing to the terms and registering. Someone on the bus without their passport finds out three steps in.

Back to a step

When “no” sends people back

Choose back to step… for the “no”. The slide draws an arrow over the top, back to that step.

Ask: will anything be different the second time round? Going back to choose again is normal. A loop is a problem when nothing changes, so people go round again and get the same answer.

From the Welsh course case: a search lists every course, advanced first. For a complete beginner the first result is a Proficiency course, so they go back and search again.

Part 2 · Making your version

Your version starts as a copy of now. Then every step from now ends up as one of four things: kept, merged, moved or removed. These are the four words the brief asks for in D2. Anything with nothing behind it is new. The Flow maker works out which is which from what you do; your job is to write why.

Kept

Still needed, still one step

One step now, one screen in your version. Renaming it, or redesigning everything on it, still counts as kept: the job is the same. Your “why” says what you changed about it.

From the missed bin case: “Summary” becomes “Check your answers”, with a Change link beside each answer. Why: fixing a mistake should not mean starting again.

Merged

Two or more steps become one

In the Flow maker, use merge ↓ to join a step to the one after it. Both old steps point to the same new screen.

From the bakery case: “What size?” and “Which flavour?”, each asked by message, become one screen of choices: size by how many people it serves, and flavour with its allergens.

Moved

Same step, different place

Use ↑ ↓. Move a step earlier when everything after it depends on the answer, and later when most people do not need it yet.

From the Railcard case: “Who is the Railcard for?” moves from after the price to first. Every later answer, from age to ID to photo, belongs to that person.

Removed

Nobody needs it on the way

Use ✕. It stays on the slide as a grey, dashed box, so a marker can see what went and read why.

The rule: a step the law or a policy requires can move or merge, never disappear. If you remove it, say where the answer comes from instead.

From the missed bin case: “Additional information” is rarely needed, so it leaves the main path and comes back as an optional box. “Assisted collection” is removed because it is already on file for the address.

New

A screen with nothing behind it

Use + Add a screen. A new screen has to earn its place: usually it prevents a ✗ further on, or it is a state you found in the week 7 check (empty, loading, error, done).

From the Railcard case: a new “Have these ready” screen lists the ID, the photo and a payment card before anyone starts. Requirements found out mid-purchase go from 2 to 0.

Six checks before you download

  1. Every yes/no question says what happens on “no”. A diamond with one way out is a step.
  2. Every loop goes back to something that will be different the second time. Otherwise it is a trap.
  3. The last box is the goal reached (“Report sent, with a date”), not “Press Submit”.
  4. Every ✗ in now is answered in your version: removed, or prevented by a screen earlier on. Say which in D2.
  5. Nothing the law or a policy requires has disappeared. It can move or merge.
  6. Someone else can follow it without asking you. Swap with a partner and see.
