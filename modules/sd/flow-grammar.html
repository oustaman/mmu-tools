Week 5 · The journey · A2, B4

Drawing a flow

A flow has a small grammar: six marks, each with one meaning. Learn them and anyone can read your journey without asking you. Today we draw how it works now, and mark where people get stuck.

Move with ← →; Present for the projector.

The idea

A flow is the order a person meets things

It is not a sitemap. A sitemap shows how an organisation stores its pages. A flow shows what one person does, one thing after another, to get one thing done.

So start from the goal. Who is it, and what are they trying to get done? “Report a missed bin collection.” Everything on the flow is on the way to that.

The grammar · 1 of 6

Step: a box with a number

Anything the person does that moves them on: reading a page, filling in a form, making a call, sending a message.

The test: would they say “then I…”? Make it one box per page, call or message. “The website” is too big. “Click Next” is too small.

The grammar · 2 of 6

Where: the small line under a step

Website, form, phone, message thread, shop counter. It looks like a detail, but every change of channel is a cost you can count.

The bakery: four channels to place one order, and the first three cannot take it. That is a finding before you have designed anything.

The grammar · 3 of 6

Wait: time when the person can do nothing

Waiting for a reply, an email, a delivery. It gets no number, because there is nothing to do. But it is counted, because it is often the longest part of the journey.

The bakery: a message on Monday at 12:44 gets its first reply on Wednesday, 46 hours later. By then Saturday is fully booked.

The grammar · 4 of 6

✗ Stuck: where people go wrong

The path is there, but people get lost, give up or make a mistake. These marks are the most important thing on the flow: they are the problems your design has to answer.

Write a fact, not an adjective. “199 words before you can start”, not “too much text”.

The grammar · 5 of 6

Diamond: the answer sends people different ways

“Yes” carries on along the row. For “no”, write what happens, and where it leads.

The test: does the answer change where the person goes next? “Which bin was not emptied?” ends in a question mark, but every answer leads to the same screen. So it is a step.

The grammar · 6 of 6

Arrow back: “no” sends people round again

Going back to choose again is normal. A loop is a problem when nothing changes, so people go round and get the same answer.

The test: will anything be different the second time round? If not, it is a trap.

Eight steps, three places people get stuck. Nobody has designed anything yet, and the job is already clear. Those three marks are what the new version has to answer.

Doing it

In the Flow maker, or on paper

  1. Write the goal.
  2. + Add a step for everything the person does, in their order. Say where it happens.
  3. + Add a wait wherever they can do nothing, and say how long.
  4. Press ✗ stuck where people go wrong, and write the fact.
  5. If an answer changes where they go, make the row a yes/no question.
  6. Download the slide for B4.

Open the Flow maker

Paper is just as good. Boxes and arrows, left to right, in a marker. Use the same six marks, and photograph it into B4. It is marked the same.

Walk it first. Do the real thing yourself, on the real service, and write down what happens rather than what the organisation says should happen.

Three checks

Before you download

  1. Every diamond says what happens on “no”. A diamond with one way out is a step.
  2. Every loop goes back to something that will be different the second time. Otherwise it is a trap.
  3. Someone else can follow it without asking you. Swap with a partner and see.
