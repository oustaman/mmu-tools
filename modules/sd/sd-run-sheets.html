Screen Design 2026–27 · weeks 1–11

Screen Design
Workshops

Every week runs on exhibits drawn from the institutes. The rooms are the slide deck — already written, already sourced, already yours — and each workshop turns one of them into a portfolio component.

Weeks 1–3 are full run sheets. Written so a colleague who did not attend the lecture can walk into one of the three rooms and run the session cold: the framing is scripted, the demo is a numbered click path, and the predictable blocks come with answers. Weeks 4–11 carry their exhibit map and assessment feed, ready to expand into sheets on the same pattern.

Two rules hold throughout: every student leaves having exported, and the swap is not a critique — it tests whether the work reads without its author standing next to it.

Week 7 needs a decision before September

The icon week was planned against a 600-record register of standardised symbols. That return was rejected whole — filled in rather than researched — and what survives is a nine-record GHS tranche read from the CLP consolidated text.

Nine is enough for the best exercise in the week and not enough for the week. The finding those nine carry is unusually good teaching: GHS mandates colour roles and no colour values at all — black symbol, white ground, red frame "sufficiently wide to be clearly visible", with no Pantone, NCS, Munsell or hex anywhere in the binding text. A student who has spent week 5 on colour tokens meeting a law that refuses to name one is the whole point of the module in a single artefact.

Three ways to fill the rest, in order of cost: (a) lean on symbolic's twelve visual-grammar entries, which are written and live and cover the hamburger, the arrow, check-and-cross and the transport set; (b) commission an ISO 7010 tranche sourced from national adopting regulations, which are free and legally authoritative where ISO itself is paywalled and bot-blocked; (c) move the icon week to the component census and teach naming disagreement instead. (a) needs no new data and is the safe default.

Verified against the content, not the map

building-map.json lists floors as full of rooms, but it is composer input, not a room inventory. The previous draft caught this once and the same check has now caught the reverse: Floor 2 was recorded as shut in that draft and is open in the build.

Counted from the live host on 27 August 2026: twenty-six authored rooms and twenty-two collections — ten Stairwell principles, seven pattern rooms, six Basement failures, two whole-system specimens and one component room. Floors 4 and R remain shut and have nothing written. Verify against the live host, never against a document — including this one, which will be wrong again.

A note on direction of linking

The module site links out to the institutes. Nothing links back. Under link-map rule 4 a topical link on a specific page is one-directional, so this is clean — but resist any impulse to add an MMU coursework link to an institute colophon. That would be exactly the reciprocal-topical shape the rule exists to prevent, and coursework is not a treaty partner.

The seed set problem, and its answer

Dropping the corpus costs one thing: the teardown instrument loses its ground-truth reading order, so week 1's Compare step has nothing to compare against. The answer is already written — the Basement's six failures and the two whole-system specimens are the worked seed set, each with published analysis and named sources. The referatory gives breadth; your own building gives the answer key. That's worth about 22 hours of not capturing screenshots.

Week1

Screen teardown

Lecture: Why screens look the way they do

2 hours
~33 students
3 rooms parallel

Exhibits

interfacesThe Basement All six documented failures — /basement/norman/ /basement/hawaii/ /basement/dark/ /basement/mode/ /basement/cookie-banner/ /basement/accidental-commerce/ The lecture's spine. Each is a failure with a named cause and a source a student can go and check — which is the difference between "this is bad" and an analysis. Hawaii is the one that lands hardest in a room of first-years.
interfacesWhole systems Bank navigation · MTR station colour Widens the frame past apps in the first hour. The MTR specimen is narrowed to station colour, which quietly models the scoping discipline students will need for their own theme next week.

Kit

Instrument: Hierarchy Teardown · Data: exhibits.json, sources.json, themes.json
Room: projector + student laptops · Print: none

Set — read this aloud

"This morning we said every screen makes an argument about what you look at first, second, third — and that it makes that argument with five tools: size, weight, colour, space and position.

Today you prove it on three real screens. Not judge them. Not redesign them. Work out what order they want to be read in, and which of the five is doing the work.

You'll finish with three teardown sheets in your portfolio and three candidate themes. You do not decide your theme today — you need three candidates, not an answer."

Phases

0:00–0:05SetRead the script. Don't extend it — the work is the lesson.
0:05–0:16DemoProject Hierarchy Teardown and work one exhibit to completion:
  • Load Norman doors from the Basement — it's the cleanest cause-and-effect in the building
  • Click elements in the order you'd read them. Numbers appear.
  • Tag each with which of the five tools makes it stand out. Some take two.
  • Compare — reveals the reading the Basement room published, with its source. Say out loud if yours differed; that's interesting, not wrong.
  • Export, and show where it lands in the spec sheet.
0:16–1:20WorkThree teardowns. First is assigned — everyone does the Hawaii alert, so the room shares a reference and sees that a hierarchy failure can have consequences past irritation. Second and third are their own, pulled from the referatory, and must be different screen types.
The prompt that unsticks nearly anyone: "if you saw this for one second, what would you have seen?"
1:20–1:40SwapPairs. Partner reads one teardown without being talked through it and says what the screen is for. If they can't tell, the teardown describes decoration rather than hierarchy — that's the finding.
1:40–1:52FixAct on the swap, then shortlist three themes from the picker. Nobody decides today.
1:52–2:00ExportEveryone exports before leaving. Check the room — unexported work is work that doesn't exist.

Say this explicitly in the room: the five tools they're naming today are the words they'll be marked on in January. Rationale §2 asks what they wanted read first and what made it happen — that is this session, three months later.

Checks

Done looks like

  • Three teardowns exported, covering at least two screen types
  • Every element tagged with at least one of the five tools
  • Three themes shortlisted — not one, not ten

If they're stuck

  • "There's no hierarchy, it's all the same." That's a finding, not a failure. Have them write why it doesn't work.
  • Redesigning instead of describing. Very common in hour one. "Not what it should be — what it is."
  • Can't pick a theme. They don't need one today. Show them too_big_variant and let them watch a huge idea get cut down.

Late enrolment

A hundred students means arrivals through clearing all fortnight. Late joiners need only the Moodle intro and today's three teardowns — there is no prior work. Say so explicitly; they assume they're behind when they aren't.

Week2

Thumbnail studies

Lecture: Composition and visual hierarchy

2 hours
~33 students
Paper session

Exhibits

interfacesWhole systems MTR station colour Composition as a system rather than a picture: one variable — colour at a station — doing navigational work across a whole network. Shows that a compositional decision scales or fails at scale.
symbolicVisual grammar The transport set · The arrow A family of marks that holds together because of shared construction rules. The direct ancestor of what a style tile does in week 6 — worth naming that forward link out loud.
interfacesThe Stairwell Hick · Miller · Fitts Three principles that explain why eight rough options beat one polished one, and why the thumbnail with fourteen elements failed before it was drawn.

Kit

Instrument: theme picker (locking) · Data: themes.json, exhibits.json
Room: tables; laptops closed until the last 10 min
Print in advance: 8-up thumbnail grids, 4 per student ≈ 140 sheets per room plus spares. Order the week before.

Set — read this aloud

"Phones away, laptops closed. For the next ninety minutes you work on paper — not nostalgia, arithmetic. You can draw eight layouts in the time it takes to make one look finished, and eight rough ideas beat one polished one every time at this stage.

Nobody is judging your drawing. Boxes and squiggles are the correct fidelity. If a thumbnail takes more than three minutes you're drawing instead of thinking.

You'll leave with eight compositions, two developed, and your theme locked. That last part is real — from next week everything you build attaches to what you choose today."

Phases

0:00–0:06SetRead the script. Hand out grids. Check everyone has a pen — across three rooms of thirty-three, someone won't.
0:06–0:14DemoDraw three thumbnails yourself, fast and badly, talking through only the hierarchy decision each time.
Do it badly on purpose. This is the highest-risk demo in the module — if your examples look competent, half the room spends ninety minutes drawing instead of thinking.
0:14–0:40Work · round 1Eight thumbnails, three minutes each. Call time every three minutes; make them move on mid-line if necessary. One screen from their shortlist, eight arrangements of the same content.
Rule: no two may lead with the same element. That constraint is what forces range.
0:40–0:50SelectLay all eight out. Pick the two most different from each other — not the two best. Say why to a neighbour.
0:50–1:20Work · round 2Develop those two larger, with real content and actual words. No lorem squiggle. 15 minutes each.
1:20–1:38SwapPairs. Partner looks at each developed thumbnail for five seconds, then says what they saw first. Compare with intent. Enforce the five seconds — the point collapses if they study it.
1:38–1:50FixAnnotate what the swap revealed, on the paper.
1:50–2:00ExportLaptops out. Photograph all ten sheets, upload, and lock the theme. The lock is a real state change — after this, changing theme is a studio conversation, not a click.

How this feeds the assessment

The concept sketches are a named requirement of the brief — this is the session that produces them. Tell the room that the six they discard are evidence too, and to photograph all eight.

Checks

Done looks like

  • Eight thumbnails, each leading with a different element
  • Two developed, with real content in them
  • All ten photographed and uploaded — not left in a bag
  • Theme locked in the spec sheet

If they're stuck

  • Drawing too carefully. Take the pen, give them a marker. Fidelity follows the tool.
  • "Out of ideas at four." Constraints, not encouragement: one with no images; one with a single button; one for someone in a hurry; one that inverts what matters.
  • Won't lock the theme. Usually fear of choosing wrong. A slightly dull theme executed well beats an exciting one abandoned in week 6.

If they missed week 1

They can do this cold. Give them a Basement room to work from instead of their own theme, and set the week 1 teardowns as catch-up before week 3 — teardowns are the one activity that genuinely doesn't work retrospectively once the module speeds up.

Week3

Define your grid

Lecture: The grid

2 hours
~33 students
Laptops required

Exhibits

writtenFloor 4 · Type Laboratory The Machinery of Text WI·MCH The page was a grid four centuries before the screen was. Columns, measure and margin as solved typesetting problems — which reframes the grid as inherited engineering rather than a web convention.
interfacesCollections Line breaking Where the grid meets real text and stops being tidy. The bridge between the historical exhibit and the thing on their laptop.

Kit

Instrument: Grid & Breakpoint Playground · Data: grids.json, sources.json
Room: projector + laptops · Bring: week 2 thumbnails — remind them on Moodle the day before

Set — read this aloud

"This morning's argument was that a grid is a decision you make once so you stop making it four hundred times. Today you make it.

You'll leave with a grid, a spacing scale and three breakpoints written into your spec sheet — and those numbers are now yours. Every screen from here obeys them, and in week 10 you write down why you chose them.

So choose for a reason. 'It looked right' is a reason, but it's a weak one, and you're the person who'll have to defend it."

Phases

0:00–0:05SetRead the script. Thumbnails on the table — this session builds directly on them.
0:05–0:18DemoProject the Grid & Breakpoint Playground:
  • Load a specimen and show its decomposition — columns, gutter, margin, max width
  • Drag the viewport handle. Name the breakpoint where the layout visibly breaks, not from a list of device sizes. This is the whole idea of the session
  • Set a spacing scale from a base unit; show the same layout beside one with a broken scale
  • Export to the spec sheet
0:18–0:50Work · gridBuild at the widest breakpoint first, then derive the narrower two. Columns, gutter, margin, max width.
Standing prompt while circulating: "why that number and not the one next to it?"
0:50–1:15Work · testThe real test. Lay one developed thumbnail from week 2 onto the new grid. It won't fit — expected, and the point. Either the thumbnail changes or the grid does; both are legitimate, but it must be a decision rather than a drift.
1:15–1:32Work · spacingDefine base unit and steps, then apply to the test layout. Every gap must be a step on the scale — no exceptions, no eyeballed values.
1:32–1:48SwapPairs. Partner sees the laid-out screen only — not the settings — and guesses the column count. If they can't, the grid isn't structuring anything visible yet.
1:48–2:00ExportExport into spec sheet §3. First assessed section completed — say so. It makes the thing concrete for the room.

How this feeds the assessment

Portfolio ← this session

Worth saying plainly: §3 is checked automatically in January — every gap in the submitted screens gets tested against the scale declared today. Not a threat, a promise that the number matters. Students who understand this in week 3 stop eyeballing spacing for the rest of the module.

Checks

Done looks like

  • §3 complete: columns, gutter, margin, max width, 3 breakpoints, spacing scale
  • Breakpoints chosen from where the layout breaks, not from a list of phones
  • One thumbnail laid onto the grid, mismatch resolved deliberately
  • Every gap is a step on the declared scale

If they're stuck

  • 12 columns because Bootstrap does. Fine, if they can say why 12. Ask what 12 divides into that 10 doesn't — if they can answer, they've got it.
  • Breakpoints named after devices. Very common. Drag the handle with them and stop where it breaks. That number is the breakpoint.
  • Thumbnail won't fit and they're distressed. Happens to everyone. Ask which is more valuable, the layout or the system — either answer is right, as long as they choose.

If they missed week 2

They can build a grid without thumbnails, but the test phase won't work. Give them a referatory screen to lay onto their grid instead, and set the eight thumbnails as catch-up — those do work retrospectively, unlike the teardowns.

Week 10 is the one to protect

If any week gets squeezed, don't let it be this one. The confidence-marker exhibit does something no textbook page will: it shows a working researcher distinguishing what I can source from what is widely repeated and unsupported — in public, on a live site, with the build refusing to ship a documented claim that names no source. Students who take that into rationale §3 and §4 write the paragraphs that get firsts. Students who don't write description and wonder why it didn't land.
