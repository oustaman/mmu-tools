Tools and AI: what they make, and what stays yours

The last lecture of this kind. From next week, this hour is me building my own deck, live, the way you build yours.

Today

  1. What you hand in, once more
  2. The tools: which ones count
  3. What AI is good at, what it can’t do for you, and five times it went wrong
  4. Using it well: your numbers in, a check, a decision, a line in the log
  5. What to be careful with
  6. Not using it
  7. What changes from next week

You hand in one PDF, with three things

  1. A clickable prototype: your screens, at one or more sizes. Add clicks to your PowerPoint, and you’re done.
  2. Process work: sketches, wireframes, mood board, style tile. Paper or software: both count. The appendices count here too.
  3. A rationale of 500–700 words: layout, type, colour, users, accessibility, AI, one ethics or sustainability point, one cross-cultural point.

Every section is on the checklist, one slide at a time. Copy a slide, paste it into your deck, fill it in.

AI is allowed, but not required

  • The brief calls AI authentic industry practice. Using it is not cheating.
  • Not using it is fine, and costs you nothing.
  • Either way, you declare it: that you used it and how, or that you didn’t. In the brief’s words: Submitting AI-generated text without declaration constitutes academic misconduct.

The tool is your choice. The decisions are what’s marked.

  • Paper and a pen: the fastest way to try a layout. A photo of it counts.
  • PowerPoint or Google Slides: your deck, and your prototype once the slides are linked.
  • Figma, Canva, anything else: welcome, never required.
  • The tools on this site: the Flow maker for B4, the Screen maker for B5, the Gallery for A3 to A5 and B3.

Nobody gets more marks for harder software. A clear wireframe on paper beats a messy one in Figma.

What AI is good at, in this module

  • Options, fast: ten palettes, five type pairings, three layouts to react to.
  • Mood board images: a feeling you can’t find a photo for.
  • A first draft of words: button labels, error messages, a help line. Then you rewrite them for your users.
  • A second opinion: “what is unclear on this screen?”
  • Your language: grammar, spelling, clarity. The argument stays yours.

What it can’t do for you

  • Know your users, or how they do it today. That came from your A2 and D1.
  • Keep twenty screens consistent. It makes one at a time.
  • Check its own work against your contrast, your sizes, your grid.
  • Give you sources it has actually read. Never cite something you haven’t seen.
  • Take responsibility. Your name is on the hand-in, not the tool’s.

    A generator makes something competent in a minute. It does not decide anything.

    The deciding is what is marked: the numbers in your A3 to A5, and the reasons in Part C. A generated palette is a suggestion until you check it and choose it.

    Put your own numbers in

    A vague prompt gets a generic answer. Give it your constraints and ask for options, not the answer.

    I’m designing a booking screen for a small bakery. Customers are mostly over 50, often on a phone. Body text is 18 px, headings 28 px. Background #FFFFFF, text #2B211C. Suggest three accent colours that pass 4.5:1 on white, and say why each one suits a bakery.

    Live

    Generate, check, decide, log

    1. Generate: ask for a palette, a type pairing or mood board images for one project.
    2. Check: against your own numbers. Is the text at least 4.5:1 on its background? Are the sizes on your scale? The contrast checker answers the first.
    3. Decide: keep it, change it, or bin it, and know why.
    4. Log: one bullet in A7. Which tool, for what, and what it gave you.

    Be careful with

    • Other people’s details: don’t paste anyone’s name, number or messages into an AI tool. The same rule as blurring them in D1.
    • Images: say where every image came from, generated or not, with a date.
    • Facts and references: check them. A confident answer can be wrong, and a reference can be invented.
    • The cost: every request uses energy. That is a ready-made point for C6, if you want it.

    A7: one bullet each time

    Says nothing

    Used ChatGPT to help with colours.

    Says what happened

    ChatGPT, to suggest colour palettes for a bakery: three palettes. I kept none, but took the idea of warm neutrals.

    C8: what you make of it

    About eighty words. Was it any good? Whose work is it? What did you change?

    The palettes were generic: all three would suit any café. Warm neutrals were worth keeping; the reds failed contrast, so the accent is mine.

    Not using AI

    • A7: one line, No AI used.
    • C8: why you chose not to, and what you did instead. It counts the same.
    • D4: one line, No AI was used in this portfolio.
    • AI built into other tools counts: Copilot, PowerPoint Designer, Canva’s Magic tools, Grammarly’s rewrites. Not sure? Log it.
