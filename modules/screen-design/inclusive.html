Design for everyone

Anyone can be in a hard situation: for good, for a while, or for a minute. Someone with one arm, someone with an arm in a sling, and a parent holding a baby all need a screen that works with one hand. Design for the hardest situation, and the screen gets easier for everyone.

The idea of situations that are permanent, temporary or situational comes from Microsoft’s Inclusive Design toolkit (2016).

How to use this page

  1. Pick three situations that fit the people who use your service.
  2. For each one, find the screen where it would be hard.
  3. Make one decision on that screen, and show it there.
  4. Copy the card into A6, and change the words in brackets to fit your service.
  5. For C7, pick one card from Different cultures, different habits, and copy its paragraph.
  6. In week 9, once your screens are finished, write C5 about the decision that helps the most people. Each card has a paragraph for it. A6 says what you decided; C5 says why it matters.

Nine situations

Can’t see the screen well

Low vision, older eyes, or anyone reading a phone in bright sun.

What makes it hard

Small or pale text, and words printed over pictures.

Decisions that help

  • Body text at least 16 px; 18 is easier.
  • Every text at least 4.5:1 against its background. The contrast checker works it out.
  • No words over photos.

Can’t tell some colours apart

Colour blindness, a black-and-white printout, a dim screen.

What makes it hard

When colour is the only clue: a red border for an error, green for done.

Decisions that help

  • Colour plus a word or an icon: “Error:”, “✓ Sent”.
  • Links underlined, not only coloured.
  • A screenshot turned grey still makes sense.

Only one hand free

An arm in a sling, holding a bag or a child, standing on a bus.

What makes it hard

Small buttons, things out of a thumb’s reach, swiping or dragging.

Decisions that help

  • Every button at least 24 by 24 px, the WCAG minimum, and the main one bigger.
  • The main button low on the screen, where a thumb reaches.
  • A tap instead of a swipe or a drag.

Reading in a second language

International students, people new to the country, visitors.

What makes it hard

Idioms, long sentences, jargon, icons with no words.

Decisions that help

  • Plain words and short sentences.
  • A word beside every icon.
  • One question per screen.

Worried, tired or in a hurry

After bad news, late at night, five minutes before a deadline.

What makes it hard

Too much at once, an unclear next step, losing what you typed.

Decisions that help

  • The most important thing first, one thing per screen.
  • A clear way back, and a way to save and come back later.
  • Error messages that say how to fix it.

Finds reading hard

Dyslexia, tiredness, a noisy place.

What makes it hard

Long lines, walls of text, words in capitals.

Decisions that help

  • Short lines: about 45 to 75 characters.
  • Headings and short paragraphs.
  • Text aligned left, in a typeface with clear letters.

New to this, or unsure with technology

Uses a screen reader

Blind people use software that reads the screen aloud.

What makes it hard

Pictures with no description, fields with no label, buttons that say “Click here”.

Decisions that help

  • A short description for every picture that matters.
  • A visible label on every field.
  • Buttons that say what they do.

Different cultures, different habits: for C7

The brief asks for one cross-cultural point (C7). Pick the one that is real for the people who use your service, make the decision on your screens, then copy the paragraph and change the words in brackets. About 70–90 words.

Names

Not everyone has a first name and a surname. Some people have one name; in China, Japan, Korea and Hungary the family name usually comes first.

What goes wrong

“First name” and “Last name” boxes make people guess, and get it wrong.

Decisions that help

  • One “Full name” field, long enough for long names.
  • Ask “What should we call you?” only if you need it.

For your C7, ready to edit

Dates and times

03/04/2027 is 3 April in the UK and 4 March in the US.

What goes wrong

A date written in numbers can be read two ways, and a missed date can cost money.

Decisions that help

  • Write the month as a word: 3 April 2027.
  • Say the time zone if people book from abroad.

Addresses and phone numbers

Not every address has a UK-style postcode, and phone numbers abroad start with a country code.

What goes wrong

Forms that only accept a UK postcode or an 11-digit number shut people out.

Decisions that help

  • Country first, then address lines that accept any address.
  • Accept +44, spaces and brackets in phone numbers.

Colour meanings

Red is a warning on a UK form; in China it is the colour of luck and celebration, and on Chinese share prices red means up.

What goes wrong

Colour alone can say the opposite of what you meant.

Decisions that help

  • Never colour alone: a word or an icon with it.
  • Check what your main colour means to the people you design for.

Icons and gestures

A US mailbox does not look like a British postbox, and a hand gesture that means OK in one country can be rude in another.

What goes wrong

An icon only works if people already know the thing it shows.

Decisions that help

  • No hand gestures as icons.

Text that grows, or runs the other way

Translated text is often longer than English: “Settings” in German is “Einstellungen”. Arabic and Hebrew read right to left.

What goes wrong

Buttons cut words off, and words inside pictures can’t be translated.

Decisions that help

  • Buttons that grow with their words.
  • No words inside pictures.

Money and numbers

In much of Europe, 1.500,00 means one thousand five hundred; the UK writes 1,500.00.

What goes wrong

The same digits can mean different amounts, and “£” is not everyone’s currency.

Decisions that help

  • Write amounts the UK way, and name the currency: £15.00 (GBP).
  • Show the total before people pay.

Titles and gender

Mr, Mrs, Miss and Ms is a British habit. Many people have no title, or one the list lacks, such as Dr or Mx.

What goes wrong

A required title or a two-option gender list makes people pick something untrue.

Decisions that help

  • Drop the title, or make it optional with free text.
  • Ask about gender only if the service needs it.

Five checks you can do yourself

No special tools, and no testing with other people. Do them on your finished screens, and fix what you find.

  1. Arm’s length. Hold the screen at arm’s length, or squint. Can you still read it, and tell what to do first?
  2. Grey. Turn a screenshot grey. Does anything only make sense in colour?
  3. One thumb. Hold your phone in one hand. Can your thumb reach the main button?
  4. Read it aloud. Is there a word you would have to explain to a friend? Replace it.
  5. Zoom. Zoom in to 200%. Does anything get cut off, or run into something else?
